# FrontierMath: Open Problems (Epoch AI; can AI solve unsolved maths problems?)

**Authors:** Epoch AI (editorial board: Thomas Bloom, Daniel Litt, Dan Romik); supported by Schmidt Sciences
**Published:** 2026-01 (pilot of 14 problems, 2026-01-27); expanded to 50 problems 2026-07-31; live page · [Source](https://epoch.ai/frontiermath/open-problems)
**Lens:** `eval-designer` · **Digested:** 2026-10-01
**Supplementary context:** the original FrontierMath paper (arXiv:2411.04872, v7 Dec 2025) for the design this page departs from; Epoch's pilot launch post (2026-01-27); the About, FAQ and Editorial Board pages; ten individual problem pages; and a third-party discovery ledger that read the problem pages on 2026-08-14 and 2026-09-14. All web sources fetched 2026-10-01. The primary source is a live page: every solved count below is stated with that date and will change.

## TLDR

FrontierMath: Open Problems (FM:OP) is Epoch AI's replacement for a question its original FrontierMath benchmark could no longer ask. The 2024 paper asked whether AI could solve very hard problems with known, machine-checkable answers and reported under 2% solved; by Epoch's own account AI was solving problems "designed to be solvable only by top human experts" by the end of 2025. FM:OP asks instead whether AI can solve research problems that professional mathematicians have tried and failed to solve, and how much each solution would matter. Every problem is proposed by a working mathematician, reviewed by a three-person editorial board (Thomas Bloom, Daniel Litt, Dan Romik), given one of four significance tiers (Moderately interesting, Solid result, Major advance, Breakthrough) and paired with a bespoke verifier program, so a candidate solution can be checked by code even though no solution is known. The pilot launched 2026-01-27 with 14 problems; the pool reached 50 on 2026-07-31; the page lists 49 as of 2026-10-01, 32 of them tagged "Construction - Finite" and 29 in number theory, Diophantine problems, combinatorics or graph theory. Epoch tests flagship models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google at release with a scaffold that provides a Python tool, a command-line tool and web search, a one-million-token output budget per sample and four samples per problem, but publishes no per-model score: a problem is retired once solved, so the headline is a first-to-solve ledger. As of 2026-10-01 it reads 9 of 49 solved: 5 of 22 Moderately interesting, 2 of 18 Solid result, 1 of 6 Major advance, 1 of 3 Breakthrough. Only 4 of the 9 carry the label "Solved (AI)"; 5 carry "Solved (human + AI)", a label that did not exist until a policy change on 2026-09-16, and both top-tier solves are in that group. The Major advance (the core in approval-based committee elections) was settled by Becker, Greger and Peters with GPT-6 Astra by proving that the requested counterexample cannot exist, so the verifier could never have accepted it; the Breakthrough (an Apéry-style irrationality proof) was met by a preprint proving ζ(5) irrational in a form the problem did not ask for, and Epoch notes the preprint "does not acknowledge particularly substantial AI use." Two of the four autonomous solves were retrieval rather than discovery: GPT-5.6 Sol found a genus-2 curve with a rational torsion point of prime order at least 31 in a public database (Epoch: "latent in the existing literature") and submitted superpermutations over 8, 9 and 10 that people had posted online the day before, one set of which was itself produced in a Codex session with GPT-5.6 Sol. A third-party ledger that read the pages on 2026-08-14 recorded 4 of 49 solved, so 5 of the 9 entered the chart in the following seven weeks (one of them, M23, by relabelling a solve dated 2026-08-11), which matches the "spate" half of board member Daniel Litt's prediction in the editorial commentary of "a spate of 'easy' problems ... and then a lull." Epoch's FAQ estimates that between 10% and 40% of the problems may be unsolvable as stated, and the changelog shows 5 problems removed since launch (one as not publishable, two because the verifiers would not detect correct solutions reliably, one for failing the notability bar after it was solved, one because a counterexample may be too large to check). Verifier access is sold, and OpenAI is the only buyer. For an eval designer: a verifier plus pre-registered significance tiers lets you count results without reading proofs, but the labels on the board (solved by whom, at which tier) are editorial decisions that moved after the fact, and the two biggest results were adjudicated by people, not by the verifier.

## Key Takeaway

The scoreboard built so that no human would have to read an AI proof records its two highest-tier solves by human judgement, because neither could pass the verifier: the Major advance solve proved that the counterexample the problem asked for does not exist, and the Breakthrough solve proved the result in a form the problem did not request, in a preprint that does not acknowledge substantial AI use. Both sit under a "human + AI" label that Epoch created on 2026-09-16, almost eight months after launch, and applied retroactively to problems already marked solved. The verifier settles the cases that matter least; the cases that matter most still end as an attribution call made by the people who run the board.

## Implications

- **Pre-register significance before the first result, and log it when the tiers move anyway**: Contributors rate each problem's notability, how many mathematicians have seriously tried (answers ranged from 2 to 4 up to 50 to 100) and time-to-solve for the most capable mathematician working full time (1 to 4 weeks up to 3 to 10 years) before any model runs, and Epoch picks the lower tier when several seem plausible because downgrading after a solve "would be disappointing." Even so, the M23 inverse Galois problem appeared under "Major advance" in the January launch post and is listed as "Solid result" today. For an eval where an agent spends a person's money, write the outcome tiers (routine, useful, remarkable purchase) into the task file before the first run and keep a dated log of every re-tiering.
- **Treat verifier acceptance as evidence, not proof, and count in two steps**: Epoch records a verifier accept on the problem page and increments the headline chart only "once a solution has been confirmed by a mathematician." On the Q2 absolute Galois problem the verifier's evidence was "short of a full proof" and the page says it "remains to check that the AI systems can actually prove this." Copy the two-step: automated check, then human sign-off, and publish both counts.
- **Build a warm-up variant with a known answer for every task and run it before you read any zero**: Epoch tests verifiers "on variants of the open problems where solutions are known," and at the pilot ran GPT-5.2 Pro and Gemini 3 Deep Think on those warm-ups to confirm they understood the instructions and the format. Three problems were still removed for verifier reasons (two because the verifiers "would not detect correct solutions with high enough fidelity," one because a counterexample may be too large to check). Budget for verifier failure as a first-class line item.
- **Decide what retrieval counts for before you add a web-search tool**: The scaffold includes web search, and 2 of the 4 autonomous solves were lookups. Epoch kept both as solved and raised the next torsion target to order 37 with the comment that the new target should be "beyond the current research frontier." For an agent buying on a live market, say in advance whether finding a listing that someone else already flagged is a win.
- **A first-to-solve ledger gives you no model comparison, so run a separate fixed set if you need one**: Four samples per problem, no per-model rates published, problems retired on solve. The only pass^k-style data is on the retired Ramsey page: in Epoch's scaffold with four samples, Opus 4.6 (max) solved it 1 of 4, Gemini 3.1 Pro 2 of 4, GPT-5.4 (xhigh) 2 of 4, and GPT-5.2 (xhigh), Opus 4.5 (max) and Kimi K2.5 Thinking 0 of 4. The 2024 paper's Table 2 shows the same spread at the easier level: on the four problems any model solved, success across five runs ranged from 0% to 100%, and only one model-problem pair hit 5 of 5.
- **Write the status vocabulary before the first ambiguous case arrives**: Launch policy was binary (solved by AI only if fully autonomous, otherwise by humans). After M23 (an author: "practically impossible to draw a sharp line") and Hadamard (three humans and Claude, marked provisionally), Epoch added "human + AI" on 2026-09-16 and told readers who need a binary to count those as AI. Define your human-involvement ladder (prompted once, steered iteratively, co-wrote the solution) on day one, and state which rung counts as "the agent did it."
- **Publish the denominator and the removals next to the score**: The page says "50 problems" and lists 49; a third-party sitemap read on 2026-09-14 found 54 problem pages (including retired and withdrawn ones); 5 problems have been removed since launch, each with a dated changelog entry. The FAQ's own estimate is that 10% to 40% of problems may be unsolvable. A solve rate is only readable against a moving pool if the moves are public.
- **Decide early who can run the verifier**: Access is sold, OpenAI is the only buyer so far, and purchasers must notify Epoch and the problem contributor of any success and share publication rights. The fee funds the benchmark but means outsiders cannot replicate an accept. For a money eval the equivalent question is whether the outcome checker is open or only you can run it.

## How to Apply It (method)

**Scenario:** You want a public board for agents that spend a person's money in a live secondary market (say, sourcing a specific used titanium frame under a budget, or clearing a parts list from marketplace listings). You are tired of leaderboards that saturate within a year, so you want the FM:OP shape: a pool of outcomes practitioners agree are hard and worth achieving, each with a programmatic checker, a first-to-achieve ledger, and a changelog, instead of a per-model percentage.

**Steps:**

1. **Write the question so it is not about AI**: Epoch's rule is that problems are "meaningful to mathematicians on their own terms. We do not select problems to be difficult for AI per se." For the market agent: tasks experienced buyers describe as hard and valuable (a rare frame size under market price inside 30 days; a full groupset from three sellers with combined shipping under a cap), not tasks engineered to trip models.

2. **Source tasks from practitioners with a proposal form**: Epoch's form captured the statement, why it matters, how many people have seriously tried, a time-to-solve guess, a probability that the task is solvable as stated (target at least 80%; many contributors answered 50% to 80%), and a verifier sketch. Ask the same of experienced buyers, and record their answers as the baseline.

3. **Convene a small editorial board and tier every task**: Three mathematicians reviewed each submission against a minimum bar and placed it in one of four tiers. Compress the definitions for your domain and keep the conservative rule:

   ```
   Tier 1 (Moderately interesting): posed at least 10 years ago, worked on by at least two
     independent teams, connected to broader questions but not a priority for a large program.
   Tier 2 (Solid result): a strong researcher would be happy if their median output addressed
     problems of this caliber; little engagement outside the subfield.
   Tier 3 (Major advance): the median person in a broad area (number theory, graph theory)
     would take note and make time to understand the outline.
   Tier 4 (Breakthrough): the median mathematician would want to know, even outside their area;
     a candidate for one of the best results of the year.
   When several tiers seem plausible, choose the lower one.
   ```

4. **Write a verifier per task and state its tolerances in the prompt**: Each FM:OP problem ships a program that runs on a typical computer; some are one-line checks, some call specialised software, and some accept "strong numerical evidence" short of proof. The Apéry prompt is the model: conditions checked for 0 ≤ n < 650, fixed C = 100 and ρ = 0.95, at most 100 decimal digits per integer, floating-point comparison against a 6000-digit value of X, m > 21 rejected outright. For the market agent the verifier checks the delivered listing against spec, price cap, seller history and timestamps, with the tolerances written into the task.

5. **Exclude tasks where consensus says no solution exists, and say how many of the rest might not**: Epoch excluded counterexamples to conjectures that are believed true (a Goldbach counterexample would tell you nothing when it fails to appear) and still estimates 10% to 40% of the pool may be unsolvable. Publish your own estimate.

6. **Build a warm-up variant with a known answer for every task**: Run the verifier on it (verifier test) and the models on it (format and comprehension test) before the open task counts for anything.

7. **Pre-register the caveats**: Epoch's launch post lists five in advance so they cannot be accused of moving the goalposts: collaborations (how much of the conceptual work was the AI's), prior art (an existing result the humans missed), conventional compute (a supercomputer-month on an AI-suggested search), verifier misspecification, and sample bias from the verifiability filter. Write your five.

8. **Fix the run protocol and publish it**: Flagship models at release; tools are Python, a command line and web search; one million output tokens per sample with compaction; four samples per problem. Decide whether web search is in or out, because with it in, two of Epoch's four autonomous solves were lookups.

9. **Count in two steps**: Verifier accept goes on the task page; the headline increments only after a human confirms. Record both dates.

10. **Define the status labels before the first grey case**: Unsolved; Solved (AI) when the core ideas are "unambiguously contributed by AI," judged largely on author attribution; Solved (human + AI) when AI "was instrumental in solving the problem, but did not solve it autonomously"; Solved (human). For record-breaking tasks, mark solved on the first break and treat new targets as unofficial.

11. **Retire on solve, and changelog every add, remove and relabel with a date and a reason**: Epoch's changelog carries seven dated entries between 2026-02-24 and 2026-09-16, including the removal of two problems whose verifiers would not reliably detect correct solutions and one whose solve did not meet the notability bar.

12. **Open a submission channel and state its limits**: Epoch takes solutions by email, runs the verifier, and says plainly that it "cannot adjudicate claims to show that a problem is unsolvable." Say what your board will and will not rule on.

13. **Decide the verifier access policy**: Public, or paid with a notify-and-share clause like Epoch's. Either is defensible; silence is not.

**Expected outcome:** A dated ledger of hard, practitioner-defined outcomes with a programmatic checker each, a tiered significance axis fixed in advance, a two-step count that separates "the checker accepted it" from "a person confirmed it," explicit labels for how much a human steered the agent, and a public changelog. It will not rank models. It will tell you, with a date, whether any agent has yet done each thing and how much that thing was worth to the people who asked for it.

## What Experts Overlook

The detail that makes a first-to-solve ledger readable is not the verifier, it is the warm-up: for each open problem Epoch holds a variant with a known solution, and both the verifier and the models are run on it first. The FAQ states that verifiers are tested "on variants of the open problems where solutions are known," and the launch post reports that GPT-5.2 Pro and Gemini 3 Deep Think "are generally capable of solving warm-up problems," which "tells us that they understand the instructions and are familiar with the subject area. It also helps test the verifiers." Without that step a zero on an open problem is ambiguous between "the model cannot do this," "the model did not understand the submission format" and "the verifier is broken." With it, a zero means the first of the three. The companion detail is the two-step count: a verifier accept is recorded on the problem page, and the headline chart increments only "once a solution has been confirmed by a mathematician." On the Q2 absolute Galois problem the page says the verifier evidence is "short of a full proof" and that it "remains to check that the AI systems can actually prove this," and the problem is nonetheless marked solved, which shows where the human step actually sits.

**Why it matters:** The changelog shows the verifier is the weakest component of the instrument even with warm-ups: two problems were removed on 2026-07-31 because the verifiers "would not detect correct solutions with high enough fidelity," one on 2026-09-16 because a counterexample might be too large for the verifier to check, and the committee-election verifier (an integer linear program that checks whether a submitted instance has an empty core) could never have accepted the solution that settled the problem, because the solution proved no such instance exists. Litt wrote in the editorial commentary: "Some of the problems may turn out to have verifiers that are measuring something weaker than what the problem is asking for, or that have some vulnerability a model can find. Human review will still be essential." The warm-up and the two-step count are the two places that review is built into the pipeline rather than left to goodwill.

**Example of good use:** For an agent buying on a live market, give every open task a warm-up with a known-good answer: a listing that definitely satisfies the spec and price cap, planted or historical. Run the checker on it to prove the checker accepts a right answer, and run the agent on it to prove the agent can produce a submission in the required shape. Only then run the open task. Record the checker's accept on the task page and increment the board only when a person confirms the purchase actually met the buyer's intent (correct size, not a counterfeit, seller delivered). Publish both dates.

**Example of misapplication:** A team skips the warm-ups because the checker "is just a script," runs four samples per task, and reports that no agent solved anything. Half the zeros are format failures (the agent returned a URL where the checker wanted a JSON object) and two are checker bugs, which nobody discovers until a human later finds a listing the checker rejects. The opposite failure is treating the checker's accept as the headline: an agent "wins" a task by submitting a listing that satisfies every field in the spec but is a scam, which is the analogue of a verifier accepting evidence "short of a full proof." Epoch's design puts a mathematician between accept and headline for exactly this reason.

## Extracted Prompts

The problem pages publish the exact prompt given to AI systems for each problem. The shorter solved-problem prompts are reproduced verbatim below, followed by the three prompts from the original FrontierMath paper (Appendix B.1), which show the design this page departs from. Where an original contains an em dash it is rendered here as a hyphen, per this wiki's style rule; no other characters were changed. The longer prompts (elliptic-curve rank, genus-2 rational points, genus-2 torsion, superpermutations, Q2 absolute Galois) are on their problem pages at `epoch.ai/frontiermath/open-problems/<slug>`.

**Prompt explanation:** Hadamard matrix of order 668 (Moderately interesting; Solved, human + AI). The shortest prompt on the board; the verifier checks orthogonality of a submitted ±1 matrix.

```
Find a Hadamard matrix of order 668.

Write your Hadamard matrix to an .npy file (using `numpy.save`) and output the filepath. The matrix must be a square array of +1 and -1 entries.
```

**Prompt explanation:** Inverse Galois problem for M23 (Solid result on the page today, Major advance in the January launch post; Solved, human + AI). Shows a size cap (coefficients under 100 digits) and a strict output grammar (Magma syntax) written into the prompt.

```
Find a degree 23 polynomial in Z[x] whose splitting field over Q has Galois group M23. The coefficients of the polynomial must have fewer than 100 decimal digits.

Provide your solution as a single polynomial expression in Magma syntax, e.g.
  3*x^2 - 2*x + 1
Only integer literals, the variable x (lower case), the operators + - * ^ and parentheses are
accepted. Do not wrap the expression in quotation marks, and do not add a trailing semicolon, an
assignment, or any other Magma code.
```

**Prompt explanation:** The core in approval-based committee elections (Major advance; Solved, human + AI). A counterexample prompt whose verifier (an integer linear program) could only ever accept an instance with an empty core; the solve proved no such instance exists.

```
Your task is to construct an approval-based committee election whose core is empty.
