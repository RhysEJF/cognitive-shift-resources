# Why There Are Complementary Learning Systems in the Hippocampus and Neocortex

**Authors:** McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly
**Published:** 1995-07 · [Source](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMcNaughtonOReilly95.pdf)
**Lens:** `memory-architect` · **Digested:** 2026-05-19

## TLDR

McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly (1995) ask why brains pay the apparent cost of running two memory systems instead of one, and the answer they construct from connectionist simulations is a hard mathematical constraint, not a biological accident: any single network that uses overlapping distributed representations to extract shared structure across experiences (semantic / neocortical learning) cannot also absorb arbitrary new facts rapidly without suffering **catastrophic interference** — the classic McCloskey & Cohen (1989) result where learning the AC list erases the AB list from a back-prop network even though humans show only mild interference. Their resolution is the **Complementary Learning Systems (CLS)** framework: a fast-write hippocampal system stores specific episodes as sparse, conjunctive, low-overlap traces (Marr 1971 archicortex; McNaughton 1989 "summary sketch") that act as a temporary buffer; consolidation is then the gradual re-presentation of those traces, **interleaved** with continued exposure to the existing corpus, into the slow-learning structured neocortex. They demonstrate the principle with three runs of Rumelhart's (1990) semantic network: teaching it "penguins are birds that swim but cannot fly" via focused learning (Figure 11a) hits the new knowledge fast but destroys what the network already knew about other birds and animals (Figure 11b); interleaving the new fact with the existing 15-pattern can-relation training corpus learns it more slowly but with **near-zero interference** on the prior knowledge. They then fit two animal consolidation studies quantitatively: a 16-input × 16-hidden × 16-output network reproduces Kim & Fanselow's (1992) 28-day consolidation curve for fear conditioning with one epoch per simulated day, and a similar fit holds for Zola-Morgan & Squire's (1990) 10-week curve for primate discrimination learning. The general theorem (Section "Why Discovering Structure Depends on Slow Learning", building on White 1989): for any **stochastic, on-line, parameter-updating procedure** that estimates population statistics from a stream of single-sample experiences, the smaller the effective learning rate, the more accurately the parameters converge to the true population statistic — and gradient-descent multilayer nets specifically require small steps to avoid undoing prior weight changes via interaction across layers. So slow learning is not a defect of back-prop, it's the mathematically necessary regime for extracting structure; the hippocampus exists to bridge the resulting acquisition speed gap. The paper also sketches the implementation: bidirectional cortex↔hippocampus pathways compress the cortical pattern into a "summary sketch", reinstatement during off-line states (sleep — they cite Buzsaki 1989 sharp-wave-ripple replay) drives interleaved re-exposure, and the temporally graded retrograde amnesia data (Squire & Cohen 1979 TV-test, Winocur 1990, Kim & Fanselow 1992, Zola-Morgan & Squire 1990) is the empirical signature that consolidation transfers traces gradually from hippocampus to neocortex over windows ranging from days to ~15 years in humans. **The lesson the entire agent-memory field inherits from this paper:** if your system has to both write fast and generalize well, you cannot do it in one component — you need a fast-write buffer + slow-consolidating structured store + an interleaving mechanism to move material between them without erasing what's already there.

## Key Takeaway

The single most important claim for anyone designing an AI memory architecture is that **fast write and structured generalisation are mutually exclusive in one representation**. Catastrophic interference (McCloskey & Cohen 1989) is not a bug of back-propagation that better optimisers will fix; it is a structural consequence of using overlapping distributed representations to share structure across patterns. The moment you commit to that representational regime — and you must, because nonoverlapping representations destroy your ability to generalise (French 1991, cited p. 433) — you have signed up for slow, interleaved learning. Any agent-memory design that tries to extract patterns AND ingest specific facts AND avoid forgetting in the same store is fighting a 30-year-old impossibility result. The brain's solution is to refuse the trade-off by running two systems with different learning rates and different representational sparsity, connected by a controlled reinstatement loop. The corresponding architectural rule for agent memory: **stop trying to make your pattern memory and your episodic memory the same thing.** Verbatim event stores (Adler-style L1, your transcript logs) and consolidated pattern files (your `memory/patterns/`, your fine-tuned weights) are not redundant — they are *required* to be separate, because consolidation between them must be (a) slow, (b) interleaved with continued exposure to the existing corpus, and (c) driven by deliberate reinstatement rather than greedy compression.

## Implications

- **Two separate memory tiers are not an implementation detail — they are forced by the math.** Any agent memory architecture that tries to unify a fast-write episodic store with a slow-extracting semantic/pattern store will suffer catastrophic interference at the moment the structured store gets large enough that new writes overlap with old ones. The brain has been doing this for ~300 million years because there is no other stable solution. Map this onto Flow OS: `experiences/captures/` and the per-session transcript logs ARE the hippocampal trace; `memory/patterns/` ARE the neocortex; the gap between them is the consolidation window. Do not merge them. `[N, E ↔ A]`

- **Consolidation must interleave new material with re-presentation of the existing corpus — not just write the new thing.** The paper's Figure 11b is the entire game: focused learning ("just train on the new fact") collapses the prior knowledge; interleaved learning ("train on the new fact mixed with the existing 15 facts") preserves it. Concrete /learn protocol change: when adding a new pattern memory, the consolidation step should **re-surface and lightly re-affirm a sample of the existing patterns from the same topic cluster**, not just append the new one. Otherwise the dedup/merge step is silently performing focused learning on the structured store. `[A → M, E]`

- **The hippocampal buffer's representational sparsity is load-bearing — verbatim is not the only requirement.** The paper emphasises that the hippocampal traces use **sparser, low-overlap representations** than the neocortex (Section "The Hippocampal Memory System", p. 423; revisited in Section on "Levels of Sparseness", pp. 451-453) precisely so that one new write does not interfere with another. For agent memory: it isn't enough to store events verbatim — events need distinguishable, low-overlap *keys* (timestamps, conversation IDs, entity stub IDs) so retrieval can pull individual episodes without smearing them into the pattern store. The single-SQLite-file Adler approach already gets this via per-row IDs; designs that immediately embed-then-aggregate (e.g. "store an embedding of the gist") collapse this property. `[N, R]`

- **Off-line consolidation belongs in the architecture as a first-class loop, not as a cron job afterthought.** The paper attributes consolidation to reinstatement during sleep states (sharp-wave-ripple replay, Buzsaki 1989; "off-line, inactive states", p. 425) — a periodic, structured re-presentation of recent hippocampal traces to the cortex. For Flow OS: a nightly "consolidation pass" that walks the last 24h of captures, samples them alongside related existing pattern memories, and writes the merged-and-refined patterns IS the correct architectural shape. The Stop-hook→/learn loop is the analogue; what's missing is the interleaving with existing memories at consolidation time. `[A, M]`

- **Consolidation is gradual on purpose — instant promotion to "the pattern" is the failure mode.** Across the modelled studies the consolidation window ranged from ~28 days (Kim & Fanselow 1992 rats) to ~10 weeks (Zola-Morgan & Squire 1990 monkeys) to **~15 years** in humans (Squire & Cohen 1979 TV-test). The temporal gradient is what makes structure-discovery possible: information that turns out to be one-off noise decays from the buffer before it ever stamps the structured store; information that recurs gets repeatedly re-presented and accumulates. **Implication for agent memory:** the strength of a pattern in `memory/patterns/` should be a function of how many times related captures have been re-surfaced over a multi-week window, not a function of "this got mentioned once and the LLM extractor thought it was a pattern." `[A, M, G]`

- **Quasi-regularity, not pure regularity, is the realistic structure to design for.** Section "Arbitrary Associations, Quasi-Regularity, and Memory for Facts and Experiences" (p. 437-439) argues that semantic, episodic, and encyclopedic knowledge are all *quasi-regular* — each fact has some structure shared with similar facts AND some idiosyncratic noise. The implication: your pattern store should not strip the idiosyncratic content (that would be Mem0-style extract-on-write); it should encode the shared structure WITH pointers to the original event traces so the idiosyncratic content can be retrieved when needed. This is exactly the L4-points-back-to-L1 invariant that the Adler paper independently re-derives 31 years later. `[A, G, R]`

- **Self-reinforcement of off-line replay is a real architectural lever — design who triggers it.** The paper notes (p. 441) that hippocampal synaptic plasticity is **suppressed during slow-wave sleep** (Leonard, McNaughton & Barnes 1987), which means spontaneous replays in irrelevant contexts may not strengthen the hippocampal trace, while task-relevant replays do. Translated: not all replays should be equal. The consolidation loop should weight reinstatement of memories that recur in task-relevant contexts (a user keeps asking about a customer → that customer's pattern gets more replay weight) over background memories. This is a knob: who decides which traces get reinstatement priority is an architectural question, not a tuning question. `[A, R, M]`

- **The 1995 quantitative fits validate the framework, not just the metaphor.** Two animal studies, one architecture (3-layer back-prop net, hippocampus as black-box training-data source), one free parameter (learning rate ≈ epochs/day), and both consolidation curves come out within error bars. This matters because most agent-memory papers offer architectures without a quantitative model of how the system should evolve over time; CLS provides one and proves it predicts. Anyone building agent memory should be able to write down the analogous prediction: *given this consolidation rate, this hippocampal decay rate, and this corpus-recurrence distribution, this is the half-life of an arbitrary fact*. If you can't write that down, you don't have a memory architecture — you have an extraction pipeline. `[M, A]`

- **The "two systems" claim is a constraint on *all* future architectures, not just the brain.** Section "Generality of the Relation Between Discovery of Shared Structure and Gradual, Interleaved Learning" (p. 437) generalises beyond connectionism: any "stochastic, on-line, parameter-updating procedure" that estimates population statistics from a stream of samples — including fine-tuning, RLHF, online-updated retrievers, anything you can imagine doing to a model's weights to make it "learn from experience" — falls under the same constraint. So if your strategy for agent memory is "we'll fine-tune the model nightly on the day's transcripts," you have re-derived the focused-learning failure case in 2026, and you will get catastrophic forgetting on whatever the model already knew that interacted with the new training distribution. The escape hatch is the same as 1995: interleave new data with replay of the prior distribution, or keep two systems. `[A, N, M]`

## How to Apply It (method)

**Scenario:** You run the memory layer for an agentic OS. Captures arrive as verbatim transcripts; a downstream extractor proposes patterns and writes them to `memory/patterns/`. You have noticed that as the pattern library grows, the LLM extractor starts producing patterns that contradict earlier ones, and previously-correct patterns get silently softened or overwritten when a single new capture seems to disagree. You want to convert your write-time extractor into a CLS-shaped consolidation loop, with measurable interference resistance, before the pattern store reaches the size where catastrophic interference becomes irreversible.

**Steps:**

1. **Freeze a "prior knowledge" eval set from your current pattern store.** Pick 30-50 patterns currently in `memory/patterns/` that you consider settled (e.g., positioning claims, established frameworks, customer-context anchors). For each, write 1-2 retrieval questions whose correct answer depends on that pattern. This is your AB list. Snapshot answers from your current system; these are the "before" values you'll use to measure interference.

2. **Identify the most likely interference source: recently-arrived captures whose patterns overlap with the AB list.** Pull the last 7 days of captures, run them through QMD vsearch against the AB pattern slugs, and select the captures whose top hit is in your AB list with score > 0.5. These are your AC candidates — new material that the extractor *will* try to push into the same representational neighbourhood as the AB patterns.

3. **Run the focused-learning baseline (Arm A).** Let your current extractor process the AC captures normally — one capture at a time, no awareness of the AB patterns it touches. After each batch of 5 captures processed, re-ask the AB eval questions. Track the answer-similarity score per AB question vs the snapshot. Plot the cumulative AB-recovery rate over captures-processed. The McCloskey-Cohen prediction: AB recovery declines sharply, especially on questions where the AC capture had high overlap.

4. **Build the interleaved-learning treatment (Arm B).** Re-run the same AC captures, but for each one, before invoking the extractor, retrieve the top-3 most-similar EXISTING patterns from `memory/patterns/` (QMD vsearch on the capture text). Pass both the capture AND the existing patterns into the extractor with the instruction: "propose a new pattern OR a revision of an existing pattern, ensuring consistency with the provided existing patterns; if the capture contradicts an existing pattern, output a CONTRADICTION record naming both rather than silently overwriting." After each batch of 5, re-ask the AB eval questions, same as Arm A.

5. **Measure interference quantitatively per arm.** For each AB question, compute interference = 1 - (current_answer_similarity / snapshot_answer_similarity). Plot interference vs captures-processed for both arms. The 1995 prediction (Figure 11b): Arm A interference rises sharply and saturates near 1.0 on the most-overlapping questions; Arm B interference stays near 0 across the board. If your Arm A interference is < 0.2 across the board, your extractor is already conservative enough that this isn't your bottleneck; if your Arm B interference exceeds Arm A on any question, your interleaving prompt is misfiring — usually because the retrieved "existing patterns" are not actually the ones that overlap with the capture and you need a better retrieval query.

6. **Add a temporal-gradient acceptance gate (consolidation, not promotion).** Patterns surfaced from a single capture should NOT immediately enter `memory/patterns/` at full strength. Implement a three-tier confidence: `provisional` (one capture), `consolidating` (≥3 captures across ≥7 days), `consolidated` (≥5 captures across ≥30 days). The pattern file's frontmatter `confidence` field is the analogue of consolidation state. Only `consolidated` patterns participate in interference checks for future Arm B runs — provisional patterns are inert. This is the operational equivalent of "hippocampal traces that don't repeatedly recur decay before they imprint on neocortex."

7. **Add the nightly interleaved-replay job.** Once daily, run a consolidation pass: walk the last 24h of captures (the "hippocampal traces"), sample 20% of the existing `memory/patterns/` weighted by topic-overlap to those captures (the "neocortical re-exposure"), and ask the extractor to merge-or-revise. Importantly, the sample MUST include patterns the captures DON'T touch but live in the same topic neighbourhood — this is what prevents the new captures from dragging unrelated nearby patterns toward their content (the equivalent of "penguins can swim" dragging "robins can swim" toward true in Figure 11b).

8. **Decay rule for provisional traces.** Provisional pattern proposals that fail to be re-instantiated by another capture within 14 days should be archived (move file from `memory/patterns/provisional/` to `memory/patterns/_decayed/`). This is the analogue of hippocampal trace decay — without it, the "fast write buffer" fills with one-off noise and starts contaminating retrieval. The 14-day window is a hyperparameter equivalent to the 28-day Kim & Fanselow rat consolidation curve at a scaled rate; adjust to your capture volume.

9. **Run the maintain-axis stress test.** Pick 10 patterns where the user has, over the past month, explicitly contradicted or refined the original claim (you can find these by grepping captures for "actually," "I was wrong about," "let me update", or by asking the user). For each, check: is the current `memory/patterns/` entry the latest version, the earliest version, or both versions linked by a `supersedes` edge? Arm A typically locks in whichever version the extractor processed first; the CLS-shaped pipeline should produce both versions linked. This is the qualitative bar for whether your consolidation loop is actually maintaining or just performing focused learning more politely.

10. **Cost & cadence check.** Track per-consolidation-pass LLM-token spend and elapsed time. The 1995 paper assumed reinstatement happened in sleep — i.e., off-line and cheap. Your consolidation should run when the user is idle, not on the hot path. If your nightly pass is taking > 30 min or spending > $1/day at current capture volume, the interleaving sample is too large; cap at top-5 most-overlapping existing patterns per capture.

11. **Write down the half-life prediction.** From the 1995 framework: given your consolidation rate (captures-consolidated/day), your decay rate (provisional patterns archived/day), and your average capture-topic recurrence rate (how often new captures land in an existing topic cluster), you can predict the half-life of an arbitrary fact mentioned once. Compute it. If it's < 7 days, your fast-write buffer is too leaky; if > 90 days, your decay is too slow and you're accumulating noise. Tune until it matches the user's intuitive sense of "how long would you expect Flow OS to remember something I mentioned once."

12. **Decision and writeup.** Tabulate Arm A vs Arm B interference, the maintain-axis qualitative result, the pattern-tier distribution after 30 days, and the predicted vs measured half-life. The CLS framework's bet is that interleaved consolidation + decay + tiered confidence will give you a maintain-axis-correct memory at the cost of slower pattern crystallisation. If that trade-off is acceptable for your workload (it is for second-brain use; might not be for a high-throughput trading agent that needs instant pattern updates), migrate the pipeline to Arm B and retire the focused-learning extractor.

**Expected outcome:** A consolidation pipeline whose interference resistance can be quantified per-pattern, whose pattern-confidence states correspond to consolidation tiers with measurable decay, and whose nightly replay job interleaves new captures with the existing pattern neighbourhood so the prior structured knowledge is preserved. The half-life prediction gives you a single scalar to sanity-check the system's behaviour against user expectations. Most importantly: the system now has a principled answer to "why don't I just have the LLM rewrite all patterns nightly?" — because that's focused learning, and focused learning is the failure mode the entire CLS theorem was constructed to avoid.

Together the two panels visualize the central tension the paper resolves: there is no setting of the focused-learning hyperparameters that gives you both fast new-fact acquisition AND preserved prior knowledge in a single overlapping-representation network. The figure matters because it operationalises the catastrophic-interference theorem (McCloskey & Cohen 1989) into a falsifiable architectural prediction, and that prediction is what motivates the entire CLS framework — and by direct lineage, every modern agent memory architecture that uses two tiers (verbatim event store + consolidated patterns).

## What Experts Overlook

The detail that almost every modern agent-memory paper citing CLS misses is the **representational sparsity asymmetry** between the two systems. Mostly, papers cite CLS for the headline ("two systems: hippocampus stores episodes, neocortex stores patterns") and stop there. But the paper makes a much sharper claim in Section "The Hippocampal Memory System" (p. 423) and in the more technical discussion of "Levels of Sparseness" (pp. 451-453): the two systems differ not just in *speed* but in *representational geometry*. The neocortex uses **overlapping, distributed representations** with high pattern-similarity between related items — this is what makes it good at sharing structure across patterns and bad at adding new items without interference. The hippocampal system uses **sparse, conjunctive, low-overlap representations** ("summary sketches" in McNaughton's term, p. 423) — this is what makes individual episode-traces distinguishable from each other without smearing. The 1995 paper explicitly anchors this in the neuroanatomy: the dentate gyrus produces sparser activation than CA3, and the perforant-path projection compresses the neocortical input into a smaller-population code (pp. 451-453, citing McNaughton 1989).

The Specific quantitative consequence: French (1991, cited p. 433) showed that you can avoid catastrophic interference *within a single network* by forcing nonoverlapping representations — but doing so destroys generalisation. CLS resolves this by having the two systems run *different* representational schemes, deliberately, and using the hippocampal-to-neocortical reinstatement pathway to translate sparse traces into the overlapping format the neocortex can absorb gradually. The hippocampus is not just "the same memory but faster" — it is **representationally engineered for one-shot orthogonal writes**, and that engineering is *what makes it a usable buffer*.

**Why it matters:** Most agent memory architectures get the two-tier shape right (transcript log + pattern store) but use the **same** representational geometry for both — typically a single embedding model applied to both raw events and consolidated patterns. This silently undoes the CLS design: when your consolidated patterns and your raw event traces live in the same embedding neighbourhood, two events whose embeddings are close get merged into one pattern, and a new event whose embedding is close to a pattern silently drags that pattern toward its content. The architectural mistake is invisible because the two stores are nominally separate files — but the representational space they share creates the interference the file-separation was supposed to prevent.

**Example of good use (memory architectures for agentic OSes):** A QMD-style brain should index `experiences/captures/*.md` and `memory/patterns/*.md` into the same hybrid search index — but use **different chunking and different embedding granularity** for each. Captures should be chunked at the utterance level (high specificity, low cross-capture similarity, sparse activations across the embedding space). Patterns should be chunked at the section or paragraph level (broader topical activation, deliberate cross-pattern similarity within a topic). At retrieval time, both stores contribute to results, but they're returned with explicit `source: capture` / `source: pattern` tags so the consumer (e.g. /think, /work, /learn) can reason about them differently — captures cite an event, patterns cite a generalisation. The encoding gate (Adler-style) should compute its novelty signal against the **pattern** store, not the **capture** store: if a new capture is "novel relative to existing patterns," it's a candidate for promotion to provisional pattern; if it's only novel relative to existing captures, it's just a new instance of an already-known type. Same retrieval substrate, two purpose-tuned representational regimes.

**Example of misapplication:** A team builds a "hippocampus + neocortex" memory architecture by pointing the same FAISS embedding store at two folders (transcripts and patterns) and running an LLM extractor on the transcripts to populate the patterns folder. They cite CLS in their methods section. What breaks: because both stores use the same embedding geometry, the LLM extractor's similarity-based dedup ("is this new pattern already in the store?") starts merging patterns that should be distinct (e.g., "Nadia is the user's partner" and "Nadia is a warm lead" get merged because they're embedding-close and both mention Nadia — the latter is stale test data the user has explicitly contradicted, but the merge silently picks one). And because the dedup also runs against the transcripts ("is this transcript already represented by a pattern?"), new transcripts whose embedding is close to an old pattern stop generating new patterns at all — the system stops learning. The CLS framework would have predicted this: they ran focused learning with overlapping representations, and got catastrophic interference. The fix isn't more LLM cleverness in the extractor; it's representational geometry — patterns and captures need to live in spaces where similar-content items are *less* close than they would naively be, exactly because that's what makes the buffer's "this is a distinct episode" signal usable.
