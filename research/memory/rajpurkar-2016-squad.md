# SQuAD: 100,000+ Questions for Machine Comprehension of Text

**Authors:** Pranav Rajpurkar, Jian Zhang, Konstantin Lopyrev, Percy Liang (Stanford)
**Published:** 2016-10 · [Source](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.05250)
**Lens:** `memory-architect` · **Digested:** 2026-05-19

## TLDR

The Stanford team built SQuAD — 107,785 crowdsourced QA pairs across 536 Wikipedia articles (drawn from Project Nayuki's top-10,000 English Wikipedia articles by PageRank, then paragraph-filtered to ≥500 chars, yielding 23,215 paragraphs), where every answer is a literal text span in the passage. Crowdworkers on Daemo/Mechanical Turk (97% HIT acceptance, ≥1000 prior HITs, US/Canada, paid $9/hr) were tasked with up to 5 questions per paragraph and had copy-paste disabled to force lexical/syntactic divergence; dev/test sets received ≥2 additional answers for human-baseline evaluation. The dataset breaks down by answer type into 32.6% proper nouns (person/location/other entity), 31.8% common noun phrases, 19.8% dates/numbers, and 15.8% adjective/verb/clause phrases — and by reasoning type (192 manually labelled examples) into 33.3% lexical-synonym variation, 9.1% world-knowledge variation, 64.1% syntactic variation, 13.6% multi-sentence reasoning, 6.1% ambiguous. A logistic-regression baseline with ~180M discretized features (lexicalized + dependency-tree-path features dominate) hits 51.0% F1 on dev/test, vs sliding-window 20.2% F1 and human inter-annotator 86.8% F1. Feature ablation: removing BOTH lexicalized and dep-path features collapses dev F1 from 51.0% to 35.8%. The headline diagnostic — and the part that should outlive the dataset — is Figure 5: logistic-regression F1 degrades monotonically from ~75% at zero syntactic divergence to ~30% at divergence 8, while human F1 stays flat above 85% across the whole range. Within four months of release Wang & Jiang (2016) closed half the model-human gap with Match-LSTM (70.3% F1).

## Key Takeaway

SQuAD's lasting contribution to memory-architecture work isn't the dataset — it's the **syntactic-divergence diagnostic**. The authors invented a metric (minimum edit distance over unlexicalized dependency-tree paths between question-anchor and answer-anchor) on which humans are *flat* across the full range 0–8 and models *fall off a cliff*. That asymmetry is the experimental signature of pattern-matching vs reasoning: when humans are insensitive to a perturbation and your system isn't, you've found a falsifiable test that your retrieval/comprehension layer is leaning on surface features. This is exactly the diagnostic that modern agentic-memory benchmarks (LoCoMo and its descendants — see [[ai-2026-memorybench-continual-learning]]) silently lack, which is why they get gamed by BM25 + raw transcripts.

## Implications

- **[ENGRAM-G/R — Grounding + Retrieval] Span-grounded answers are the right primitive for verifiable memory recall.** SQuAD's hard rule "the answer is a literal span in the passage" is what made automated evaluation possible at 100k scale. For a memory system: when an agent claims a fact, force it to return the *span in the source memory* that backs the claim, not a paraphrase. Paraphrases are unverifiable; spans are not. This is also why retrieval-augmented systems with citation modes work: they ship the span alongside the answer.

- **[ENGRAM-R — Retrieve] Sentence selection ≠ answer extraction; budget for both.** The paper's logistic regression picks the correct sentence 79.3% of the time but only achieves 51.0% F1 — meaning **the bulk of error sits in selecting the exact span inside the correct sentence**, not in retrieving the right passage. For memory architectures this is the gap between "we found the right memory chunk" and "we found the right phrase inside the chunk." If your evaluation only measures the former (top-k retrieval recall), you're missing two-thirds of the actual error budget.

- **[ENGRAM-E — Encode] Disable copy-paste at write time.** The annotation interface deliberately disabled copy-paste from the source paragraph, forcing crowdworkers to paraphrase. This is a write-time encoding choice that *creates* the syntactic divergence the dataset is famous for. The memory analog: if your `/learn`-style extractor is allowed to verbatim-copy from the transcript, you bake in surface-form retrieval and never measure semantic-level recall. Forcing the extractor to restate the memory in its own words is a feature, not a bug.

- **[ENGRAM-R/A — Retrieve + Aggregate] Syntactic divergence is the cleanest "is my system actually reasoning?" probe we have.** Use it as an internal eval for any memory pipeline: take a corpus, generate questions whose answers require traversing dependency paths of edit distance 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 from question to answer sentence, and plot F1 vs divergence. A flat curve = the system is doing some form of structural matching; a downward curve = it's pattern-matching surface tokens. This is far cheaper than collecting another LoCoMo-style benchmark.

- **[ENGRAM-G — Ground] Multi-annotator ground truth is non-negotiable for any "human baseline" claim.** SQuAD treats the *second* crowdworker's answer as the model prediction and the rest as ground truth — that's how they get 86.8% F1 for humans (not 100%). Any memory-system paper that claims "human baseline X" without a multi-annotator pool is reporting an inflated ceiling. For your own evaluations: budget for ≥3 annotators per question.

- **[ENGRAM-M — Maintain] The "easy" answer types (numbers/dates: 8.9% + 10.9% = 19.8% of data) inflate aggregate scores.** Logistic regression hits 72.1% F1 on dates vs 31.2% on verb phrases — a 41-point spread. Aggregate F1 hides which reasoning capabilities a memory system has actually acquired. When tracking a memory system's capability over time, stratify by answer/question type so you can see *which* skill is improving rather than just "the number went up."

- **[ENGRAM-A — Aggregate] Constituency parsing is a 77.3% ceiling on extractive recall.** Only 77.3% of correct answer spans are exact constituents in the parse tree. So if your memory system extracts only "clean" constituent spans, you've capped your recall at ~77% before you even start. Either widen the candidate space (n-grams, sub-trees) or accept the ceiling explicitly. Many "extractive RAG" systems silently inherit this cap without measuring it.

- **[ENGRAM-N — Network] Wikipedia-PageRank sampling biases toward famous topics.** SQuAD's 536 articles were sampled from the top-10k by PageRank — meaning the dataset over-represents well-trodden, well-edited topics where Wikipedia is high-quality. A memory benchmark drawn from production logs (long-tail, noisy, contradictory) would behave very differently. Generalizing SQuAD-tuned memory-retrieval techniques to user-personal corpora is non-trivial and routinely underestimated.

## How to Apply It (method)

**Scenario:** You are running an experiment on a memory pipeline for an agentic OS. Users dump messy transcripts into the system; later, sub-agents pose questions ("when did Dana sign the Flow OS contract?", "what objections did Marcus Webb raise about the integration setup pacing?"). You want a falsifiable per-pipeline diagnostic — not just "does it answer right" but "is it reasoning or pattern-matching?". You'll borrow SQuAD's syntactic-divergence stratification to build it.

**Steps:**

1. **Curate a closed-world test passage set**: From your own memory store, sample 30–50 source documents (session transcripts, captures, contact memories). Filter to docs longer than ~500 characters so there's room for non-trivial questions. Tag each with its slug.

2. **Crowdsource (or self-generate) ~5 questions per passage**: For each passage, write 3–5 questions whose answers are *literal spans* in the passage. Critical rule: ban copy-paste — every question must paraphrase the passage. This forces lexical and syntactic divergence. If using an LLM to generate questions, prompt it explicitly:

   ```
   You are reading the passage below. Generate exactly 5 questions whose
   answers are literal text spans in the passage. Constraints:
   1. Do NOT copy any 4-word phrase from the passage into the question.
   2. Use synonyms or paraphrases of the passage's wording.
   3. For each question, return:
        Q: <the question>
        A_SPAN: <the exact substring of the passage that answers it>
        A_START: <character offset where the span begins>
   Passage:
   <paste passage here>
   ```

3. **Collect 2+ additional answers per question**: For dev/test items, have a second annotator (human or different LLM) re-answer each question given *only the question + passage*. Use the second answer as "human prediction" and the others as "ground truth" — this gives your human-baseline F1. Anything above this is suspect.

4. **Compute syntactic divergence per question** (this is the heart of the method): For each question Q with answer span A in sentence S:
   - Parse Q and S with a dependency parser (Stanford CoreNLP, spaCy, etc.).
   - Find the *anchor* — a word-lemma pair that appears in both Q and S.
   - Extract the unlexicalized dependency path from the anchor to the wh-word in Q (call this path P_Q) and from the anchor to A in S (call this path P_S).
   - Compute the **minimum edit distance** (insertions/deletions only on path tokens) to transform P_Q into P_S.
   - Take the minimum over all valid anchors. That's the divergence for this question.

5. **Run your memory pipeline on every question**: Record exact-match and F1 per item. Stratify the results by divergence bucket (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6+).

6. **Plot the diagnostic curve**: X-axis = syntactic divergence; Y-axis = pipeline F1 and human F1. Look for the shape: **flat = reasoning, downward = pattern-matching**. Quantify with the slope of the linear fit.

7. **Iterate the pipeline**: If the slope is steep, try interventions that should help reasoning (better synonym handling, query rewriting, semantic retrieval over BM25). Re-plot. The slope itself becomes your KPI — not raw F1.

**Expected outcome:** A per-pipeline "reasoning fingerprint" plot. You can ship this in pipeline release notes ("v3 has slope −1.2 F1/divergence, down from v2's −4.8") and use it to falsify the claim "my new memory architecture is actually understanding context" without spending another month curating a fresh LoCoMo-style benchmark. This is a much sharper diagnostic than aggregate retrieval recall.

## What Experts Overlook

Most readers come away from SQuAD remembering "100k QA pairs" or "logistic regression hit 51 F1." What they almost always miss is the **77.3% constituency ceiling** buried in §5: only 77.3% of the gold answer spans are exact constituents in the Stanford CoreNLP parse, so the candidate-generation step (which only considers constituent spans, for tractability) imposes a hard 77.3% ceiling on accuracy *before any modelling happens*. The 51.0% F1 number is being measured against a system that has already discarded ~23% of the achievable answers. Without this constraint the numbers don't make sense — and the paper's own analysis is honest about it ("This places an effective ceiling on the accuracy of our methods").

**Why it matters:** For a memory architect, this is a direct cautionary tale about candidate-space pruning. Every retrieval/extraction system makes a candidate-generation choice — chunks of N tokens, sentences, paragraphs, sub-trees, named entities — and that choice silently caps your achievable recall. The 77.3% number is a clean example of a ~20% performance ceiling that *no amount of better ranking* can break through. If you don't measure the ceiling, your improvements look bigger than they are and you can't tell when you've actually maxed out the framing.

**Example of good use:** In a memory pipeline that retrieves "memory cards" (paragraph-sized chunks), explicitly run a sweep: chunk-size 100 / 250 / 500 / 1000 tokens, and for each compute *oracle recall* — assume a perfect ranker, what fraction of gold answers are reachable? Plot oracle-recall vs chunk-size. Pick the smallest chunk size whose oracle recall is above your aspiration (say 95%). Now you know your ceiling is 95% and you stop blaming the ranker when you can't push past it.

**Example of misapplication:** A team launches a new memory architecture, reports "we improved retrieval F1 from 42% → 48%, a 14% relative gain," and ships. But their candidate generator (top-3 BM25 chunks of 512 tokens) has an oracle recall of only 60% on their eval set — meaning their absolute ceiling is 60%, they're already at 80% of ceiling, and the "improvement" is mostly noise. They spend the next quarter tuning the ranker for diminishing returns when the real lever is widening the candidate set. The same pattern plays out endlessly in RAG systems that report only end-to-end F1 with no oracle-ceiling decomposition.
